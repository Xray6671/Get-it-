Lesson 01 Nevada Business BasicsAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 01 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

Every Nevada business generally needs a state business license from the Secretary of State, filed through SilverFlume, plus registration of the business entity itself if it is an LLC or corporation. Sole proprietors and general partnerships using their own legal names may skip entity formation, but almost everyone still needs the state business license.

Why does it matter?

  • Operating without the required state business license can mean penalties and can hold up city or county licensing.
  • The entity type you choose (sole proprietor, LLC, corporation) affects liability, taxes, and paperwork going forward.
  • Getting the basics right up front makes every later filing — the annual list, renewals — easier to track.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Articles of Organization or Incorporation (if an entity)
  • 📄 State Business License certificate
  • 📄 EIN confirmation letter

Lesson 02 Business Licenses & Local LicensesAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 02 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

Beyond the state business license, most cities and counties — Las Vegas, Henderson, Clark County, and others — require their own local business license before you open. Requirements, fees, and renewal cycles vary by jurisdiction and sometimes by industry.

Why does it matter?

  • Operating without a local license can mean fines or an order to stop operating.
  • Some professions or locations need additional permits — health, zoning, signage — on top of the basic license.
  • Local licenses usually renew annually, on their own schedule, separate from the state license.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 City or county business license certificate
  • 📄 Any special-use or zoning permits
  • 📄 Local license renewal notices

Lesson 03 DBA / Fictitious Business NameAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 03 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

If a business operates under a name other than the owner’s own legal name, or the entity’s registered name, Nevada requires a Fictitious Firm Name certificate. It is filed with the County Clerk in the county where the business operates — not with the Secretary of State — and filing it does not give exclusive rights to the name.

Why does it matter?

  • Banks typically require a filed DBA certificate (Doing Business As, i.e. a fictitious business name filing) before opening a business account in the operating name.
  • Using a name without filing it can create confusion about who actually owns the business.
  • A fictitious firm name certificate is only valid for a set number of years and has to be renewed.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Filed Fictitious Firm Name certificate
  • 📄 County Clerk filing receipt

Quick Check

Does your business operate under a name that is not your own legal name or your entity’s registered name?

Lesson 04 Business Address & Registered AgentAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 04 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

An LLC or corporation registered with the Nevada Secretary of State must continuously maintain a registered agent with a physical Nevada street address who can accept legal documents on the business’s behalf (Nevada Revised Statutes, NRS 78.090 for corporations, with similar rules for LLCs). This is separate from the business’s own operating address.

Why does it matter?

  • Nevada law can penalize an entity that fails to maintain a registered agent, and it can affect good standing.
  • Legal notices and lawsuits go to the registered agent — missing one can mean missing a deadline to respond.
  • Sole proprietors do not need a registered agent, but should still keep a reliable address on file with their licenses.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Registered agent confirmation or service agreement
  • 📄 Proof of current address on file with the Secretary of State

Quick Check

Is your registered agent information current with the Secretary of State?

Lesson 01 Do I Need Workers’ Compensation Insurance?About 6 minutes

Lesson 01 · About 6 minutes · Free

Why this matters

Nevada generally requires employers covered by the workers’ compensation laws to provide and secure workers’ compensation coverage for employees. There are limited exceptions, and the answer can depend on the nature of the business, the people performing the work, and the circumstances of the relationship. (Nevada Legislature)

Don’t guess based only on a job title or how someone is paid.

The important question is:

Does Nevada law require my business to provide workers’ compensation coverage for the people working for me?

Lesson 02 What Happens If I Don’t Have Required Workers’ Compensation Insurance?About 4 minutes

Lesson 02 · About 4 minutes · Free

Don’t wait until there is an injury.

Nevada employers who fail to secure and maintain required workers’ compensation coverage can face significant consequences.

Nevada DIR identifies possible consequences including administrative fines, premium penalties, responsibility for costs associated with an uninsured claim, and possible criminal prosecution. (Nevada Directory)

A potential enforcement path

The circumstances can vary, but an uninsured employer may encounter a process involving:

1
Coverage issue identified

The Division may determine that an employer did not have required workers’ compensation coverage.

↓
2
Records and information are reviewed

The business may need to provide information concerning its operations, employees, payroll, insurance coverage and the period of alleged noncoverage.

↓
3
Administrative action

Depending on the circumstances, the employer may face administrative penalties and other enforcement action.

↓
4
Stop-work / cessation order

Nevada law provides authority for an order requiring business operations to cease when applicable requirements are not met. Nevada regulations describe enforcement of an order of cessation at a place of employment or jobsite. (Nevada Directory)

↓
5
Financial consequences

An uninsured employer may face an administrative fine and a premium penalty for the period without coverage. Nevada DIR currently explains that an employer who fails to secure and maintain a workers’ compensation policy may be charged an administrative fine of up to $15,000. (Nevada Directory)

↓
6
If an employee is injured

The financial exposure can become substantially more serious. An uninsured employer may be responsible for costs associated with the claim. Nevada law also provides mechanisms for recovery of amounts paid from the Uninsured Employers’ Claim Account, including applicable costs and interest. (Nevada Legislature)

↓
7
Possible criminal prosecution

Nevada DIR identifies possible criminal prosecution through the Attorney General’s Office as another potential consequence of failing to maintain required coverage. (Nevada Directory)

The important lesson

Workers’ compensation should not be treated as a document you check once a year.

Lesson 05 DIR Fines & a Cancelled Workers’ Comp PolicyAbout 4 minutes

Lesson 05 · About 4 minutes · Free

What is it?

Nevada’s Division of Industrial Relations (DIR) can charge an employer that fails to secure and maintain workers’ compensation coverage for the back premiums it would otherwise have owed a private carrier — calculated for up to 6 years of non-compliance — plus interest (NRS 616D.200). (Nevada Revised Statutes) A cancelled policy that leaves a business without coverage, even briefly and even by accident, can trigger this same exposure.

Why does it matter?

  • Back premiums plus interest, calculated over as much as 6 years, can add up to a large and unexpected bill — this is an administrative charge the Administrator can assess, separate from any court case.
  • If a workplace injury or death occurs during a period without required coverage, a first offense can be charged as a category C felony: 1 to 5 years in prison and a fine of “not less than $1,000 nor more than $50,000.” A second offense within 7 years carries the same range. (NRS 616D.200)
  • A court can also order the employer to reimburse the insurer’s costs and to repay Nevada’s Uninsured Employers’ Claim Account for every benefit, administrative cost, and attorney’s fee it paid out on a claim during the uncovered period.
  • Coverage usually doesn’t disappear without warning. Under NRS 616B.033, an insurer canceling a policy for a reason other than non-payment of premium must give the employer written notice at least 30 days before the cancellation takes effect, delivered in person, by first-class mail, or electronically, and stating the effective date. (Nevada Revised Statutes) Cancellation for non-payment is not subject to that 30-day notice.

Steps if you receive a cancellation notice

1
Read the notice carefully

Check the stated effective date — if it’s for a reason other than non-payment, Nevada law requires at least 30 days’ written notice before that date.

↓
2
Contact the carrier or agent

Reach out immediately to confirm the exact reason (non-payment, underwriting decision, non-renewal, agency change).

↓
3
Pay past-due premium if applicable

If it’s for non-payment and you intend to keep the policy, pay right away — non-payment cancellations are not subject to the 30-day notice rule and can take effect faster.

↓
4
Line up replacement coverage

Shop for and bind a new policy so its effective date lines up with or precedes the old policy’s cancellation date — a same-day handoff avoids any gap in coverage.

↓
5
File the new certificate and keep records

Send the new certificate of insurance wherever it needs to go (a general contractor, the State Contractors Board, a bonding company) and keep both the cancellation notice and the new binder in your compliance file.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 The cancellation or non-renewal notice itself, with the date it was received
  • 📄 Proof of replacement coverage, including its effective date
  • 📄 Any correspondence with DIR, the designated agent, or the carrier about a coverage gap

Lesson 01 Contractor License BasicsAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 01 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

Contractors generally need a license from the Nevada State Contractors Board (NSCB) in the right classification before bidding or contracting for construction work. The NSCB is separate from DIR, and issues licenses for two years at a time.

Why does it matter?

  • Hiring or working as an unlicensed contractor can carry criminal exposure and voids certain protections.
  • A license only covers specific classifications and a monetary limit — going beyond either can void a bid or contract (NAC 624.640).
  • A license can be Active or Inactive; only an Active license may bid or contract for work.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 NSCB license printout
  • 📄 Bond documentation
  • 📄 Workers’ comp proof or exemption affidavit

Quick Check

Do you know the license classification and monetary limit for the contractors you work with?

Lesson 02 Verify a Contractor LicenseAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 02 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

Before work begins, a business can look up a contractor’s license status directly on the Nevada State Contractors Board’s public license search to confirm it is Active, in the right classification, and covers the size of the job.

Why does it matter?

  • A lapsed, inactive, or wrong-classification license can void the protections a license is supposed to provide.
  • Verifying takes a few minutes and can prevent a much larger dispute later.
  • The public record also shows bond status and any disciplinary history.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Screenshot or printout of the NSCB license lookup
  • 📄 Date the license was verified

Lesson 03 License Classification & ScopeAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 03 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

An NSCB license only authorizes work in its specific classification — general engineering, general building, or a specialty trade — and up to its monetary limit. Work outside either can void a bid or contract under NAC 624.640.

Why does it matter?

  • A contractor licensed for one trade is not automatically licensed for another.
  • A job priced above the contractor’s monetary limit is a compliance problem even if the contractor is otherwise in good standing.
  • Confirming scope up front avoids a mid-project surprise if a permit office or inspector flags it.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 License classification printout
  • 📄 Bid or contract describing the scope of work

Quick Check

Does this contractor’s license classification match the work being performed?

Lesson 04 Insurance & Certificate of InsuranceAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 04 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

A certificate of insurance (COI) is a short document from an insurer or agent that summarizes a contractor’s or vendor’s active coverage — commonly general liability and workers’ compensation — without being the full policy itself.

Why does it matter?

  • A COI is what most businesses actually collect and keep on file; the full policy is rarely needed unless there is a dispute.
  • An expired or unverified COI provides no real protection, even if it looks official.
  • Coverage limits on the COI should match what the contract or job actually requires.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Certificate of insurance
  • 📄 Any additional-insured endorsement
  • 📄 Correspondence confirming coverage with the agent or insurer

Quick Check

Do you have a current certificate of insurance on file for this contractor or vendor?

Lesson 01 Licenses & PermitsAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 01 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

Most businesses juggle more than one license or permit at a time — a state business license, a city or county license, industry permits, and sometimes a professional or trade license. Each one has its own renewal cycle.

Why does it matter?

  • Tracking licenses individually — in email, in a drawer — makes it easy to lose track of one when there are several.
  • A lapsed license can affect other approvals that depend on it, like a bank account or a bid.
  • A single list of every license and its renewal date is the simplest way to stay ahead of them.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Copy of each current license or permit
  • 📄 Renewal confirmation for each

What is it?

Document control means having one place — physical or digital — where the current version of every compliance document lives, so anyone in the business can find what is needed without hunting through email.

Changes like a new address, a new owner, added employees, or a new business activity often trigger updates to existing licenses and filings — not just new filings going forward.

Lesson 04 Expired, Cancelled & Inactive StatusAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 04 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

A license or policy can show as Expired, Cancelled, or Inactive for reasons ranging from a missed payment to a status the business chose on purpose, like an inactive contractor license. Knowing which one applies matters before assuming the worst.

Why does it matter?

  • Inactive is not always a problem — some businesses choose it during a slow period — but it does mean you cannot operate under it until reactivated.
  • Cancelled or Expired from a lapse usually needs prompt attention.
  • The status shown on a public lookup does not explain why — that takes a call to the agency or insurer.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Screenshot or printout of the current status
  • 📄 Correspondence with the agency about the status

5 lessons in this section

Lesson 01 Compliance Red FlagsAbout 3 minutes

Lesson 01 · About 3 minutes · Free

What is it?

Certain signs are worth a closer look on their own — a license status that does not match what you expected, a document you cannot locate, or a renewal that quietly came and went without confirmation.

Why does it matter?

  • Red flags are often the first sign of a bigger issue, while it is still small and fixable.
  • Catching one early is far less costly than an agency finding it first.
  • Not every red flag means something is wrong — but every one deserves a look.

What should I check?

What should I keep?

  • 📄 Documentation once the item is resolved

What is it?

Lesson 01 What Nevada’s Safety & Heat Law RequiresAbout 5 minutes

Lesson 01 · About 5 minutes · Free

Three numbers to remember: 11, 26 and 30.

Two Nevada laws work together. NRS 618.383 requires a written safety program and, for larger employers, a safety committee. The heat rule, Regulation R131-24, adds heat protections to that same program. Nevada OSHA began enforcing the heat rule on April 29, 2025.

11+

More than 10 employees

You need a written workplace safety program that includes a training program.

26+

More than 25 employees

You also need a safety committee with employee representatives, paid for their committee time.

30

Heat over 30 minutes

If most workers in a job are in the heat more than 30 minutes of any 60, not counting breaks, your program needs heat provisions.

Also required

  • The program must be given and taught in a language and format each employee understands.
  • If you use workers from a temporary staffing agency, train them on safety before they start at each site, or as soon as possible after.
  • A business that manufactures explosives needs the program and a committee at any size.

Who the heat rule does not cover

Employees who work indoors in a working climate-controlled space, including a vehicle with working air conditioning. If the air conditioning fails, you must try in good faith to fix it and protect workers in the meantime.

Employers with 10 or fewer employees don’t have to write a program, but every employer must still keep workers safe from heat.
